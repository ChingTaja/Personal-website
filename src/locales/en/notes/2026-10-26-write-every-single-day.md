title: How to write every single day (even if you have no ideas)
---

### 1. Writing is a skill you can train

Writing is about more than beautiful prose It helps you organize ideas, think clearly, and observe yourself and the world around you

Common difficulties with journaling include：

- Having no idea what to write
- Feeling your thoughts are too poor or unimportant to record
- Stopping when your writing falls short of expectations
- Giving up after a few days or a week

**The solution is to build a simple, repeatable writing process instead of forcing yourself to produce great work**

Just as fitness requires basic movements and a training plan, writing needs basic exercises

When you no longer have to decide what to write and how to begin every time, consistency becomes easier

#### Basic principles

1. You do not have to write well from the start
2. There is no rush to organize, revise, or polish
3. You are writing for yourself, so other people's opinions do not matter here
4. Build the habit first, then improve your skill
5. Handwriting is especially recommended, but a computer or voice memo also works

### 2. Three basic writing exercises

#### Exercise one：Observation

**Purpose：Train your attention and notice the details of the real world again**

This is the most basic exercise, especially when you have nothing to write about or feel anxious or mentally scattered

**How to do it**

1. Take out a notebook and pen
2. Look at your immediate surroundings
3. List what you notice through sight, hearing, smell, and other senses
4. Record what you observe without rushing to add feelings, explanations, or judgments

**Examples**

- A man is sitting next to me
- His perfume smells strong
- The train has been stopped for five minutes
- The walls are white
- The sky outside is gray

**What to focus on**

**Record what happened before explaining what it means**

For example：

- Observation：“The train has been stopped for five minutes”
- Interpretation：“The train will not move, and it makes me feel like today is going terribly”

Start by practicing the former

This does not mean observation is entirely free of subjectivity You cannot record everything, and the details you choose reveal where your attention goes

**When is it useful?**

- When you do not know what to journal about
- When you feel anxious, overwhelmed, or unable to organize your thoughts
- While commuting, waiting, or resting
- When you want to become more sensitive to everyday details

Consistently recording simple, accurate observations gradually builds writing skill and confidence

#### Exercise two：Reaction / Continuation

**Purpose：Begin with an observation and let your thoughts develop into a continuous stream**

The first exercise lists independent observations The second starts connecting them

**How to do it**

1. Write a simple sentence
2. Write your reaction to it
3. Let one thought lead naturally to the next
4. Keep writing without stopping to edit or judge
5. Fill a page or two, or continue until you temporarily run out of thoughts

**Example**

Starting sentence：

“I am late for work”

Then keep going：

“I am late for work, why is the train still not moving, I am starting to worry about what my manager will say when I arrive, I was already exhausted yesterday, and this morning I missed my alarm again, have I been under too much stress lately, why does everything seem to be piling up…”

You do not need to plan the order or make your sentences complete, elegant, or logical

**What to focus on**

- Include feelings such as nervousness, fear, or irritation
- You do not have to solve the problem immediately
- Do not delete a thought because it seems childish or unreasonable
- Do not stop to consider how to make it sound better
- This is private writing rather than a piece prepared for publication

The creator refers to George Saunders's view of writing and creativity：write something, react to it, and repeat As the process continues, the work becomes richer and more vivid

At this stage, simply keep writing without pursuing beautiful prose

#### Exercise three：Narrative

**Purpose：Describe how events unfold, understand changes within an experience, and create some distance from your emotions**

The first two exercises develop observation and continuity of thought The third begins organizing events into a narrative

**How to do it**

1. Choose a recent event you do not fully understand yet
2. Try writing in the third person, using “he” or “she” instead of “I”
3. Describe how the event began and what changed next
4. Notice how you changed during the experience

**Example**

> John was late for work today
>
> He arrived at the station five minutes later than usual because he had not gotten up when his first alarm rang
>
> He still completed his twenty morning push-ups, but the kettle broke, so his tea was not ready on time
>
> At the station, the train stopped for another five minutes He began worrying about how his manager would view his lateness

This example establishes events, background, and development without elaborate language

**Why use the third person?**

Turning your experience into someone else's story creates a little psychological distance You can observe the whole situation from the outside instead of remaining immersed in your feelings

**The key to narrative：Change**

The creator suggests that a fundamental change within the story is one important distinction between narrative and other forms of writing

For example：

- You begin anxious about being late, then realize there is no need to worry so much
- You initially want to control everything, then accept that events may not follow your plan
- You begin anxious and end in greater confusion without finding peace

A story does not require a positive ending or personal growth What matters is noticing how the experience develops and whether you change

### 3. How the three exercises differ and connect

| Exercise | Core question | Writing approach | Skill developed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | What do I see and hear? | List observable details | Observation and attention |
| Reaction | How do I react to this? | Keep extending your thoughts | Free expression and continuity of thought |
| Narrative | How did this unfold? What changed for me? | Organize events and transitions | Storytelling and self-understanding |

The exercises build on one another：

**Observation → Reaction → Narrative**

First record the external world, then your response, and finally explore how events develop and change

You do not need to complete all three every time Start with simple observation if that is enough

### 4. The creator's advice for everyday writing

#### 1. Do not treat every session as a finished work

Journaling is different from writing an article, and every entry does not need to be worth sharing

Just as you do not need to post every gym session online, daily writing is about developing your ability rather than becoming famous

#### 2. Do not pursue polished language too early

Simple sentences can create vivid images

There is no need for complex rhetoric, ornate adjectives, or abstract expressions Accurately describing what you notice already has value

#### 3. Writing can provide an emotional outlet

When stress, anxiety, or tangled thoughts accumulate, writing gives them somewhere to go

You do not have to find answers immediately or require yourself to feel calm afterward

#### 4. Consistent practice matters more than writing well at first

The creator recommends beginning with observation With repetition, your body and mind become familiar with the process, and you may begin looking forward to the sense of release

#### 5. Observation itself can provide story material

You do not always need to invent something interesting

Paying close attention to daily life reveals overlooked details, people, and events that can become material for a narrative

### 5. A daily writing routine you can start now

#### Step one：Observe for 3–5 minutes

Record five things you can currently see, hear, or notice

Do not rush to explain your feelings or the reasons behind them

#### Step two：React for 5 minutes

Choose one observation and write your thoughts, associations, and feelings

Do not stop to revise or judge the quality of your writing

#### Step three：Narrate, optionally

If something happened today that is worth revisiting, describe its development in the third person

Ask yourself：Where did it begin? What changed? Where did it end?

**The most important principle**

**Write first, understand afterward；build the habit first, pursue quality afterward**

You do not need profound insights every day or a conclusion for everything Sometimes observing life and putting your thoughts on paper is enough to complete today's practice
