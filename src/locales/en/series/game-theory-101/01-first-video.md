### What is game theory?

Put simply, game theory studies strategically interdependent behavior

Strategic interdependence means that my actions affect your outcome, and your actions affect mine

These situations can be about winning and losing

When we hear the word “game,” we usually think of competition\
One side wins and the other loses

But game theory applies more broadly

It studies not only competition, but also how both sides can help each other, win together, and interact in situations somewhere in between

In other words, we have incentives both to cooperate and to outperform each other

### Why learn game theory?

Learning game theory takes time and rigorous logical thinking

**First, it is a theory of interactive behavior**

The logic behind strategically interdependent situations can become complicated very quickly

To build logically sound theories, we need a method that ensures our conclusions actually follow from our assumptions. Game theory provides those analytical tools.

**The second reason is practical: understanding these models helps us quickly recognize similarities between different situations**

Once we invest time in learning the basic concepts\
We can connect situations that seem unrelated\
And think more flexibly and respond more quickly across different contexts

### What will the course cover?

The series is divided into three chapters

**Chapter 1: Simultaneous-Move Games**

Players must choose their strategies independently, without first observing what the others have done.

Examples include:

- Prisoners deciding what to do while being questioned separately
- Deciding whether to stop at a traffic light

This chapter introduces strict dominance, iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, best responses, mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, and weak dominance

**Chapter 2: Extensive-Form Games**

Unlike the first chapter, players take turns acting\
Examples include war and invasion plans, and police searches

This chapter introduces backward induction, subgame-perfect equilibrium, credible threats, commitment problems, and forward induction

**Chapter 3: Advanced Strategic-Form Games**

This extends the first chapter and explores deeper questions

For example: if a soccer player's accuracy when kicking left improves, will the player also choose to kick left more often?

This chapter introduces comparative statics, critical equilibria, and symmetric zero-sum games

### Math requirements and grading

The course requires logical thinking, but usually no mathematics beyond high-school algebra

One semester of calculus helps. Without it, you may only miss roughly the final 5% of the material

The speaker mentions receiving Bs and Cs in high-school math himself. He believes that ordinary learners can master the material if they put in the time and effort

As for grading, this is an online course, so there is no need to take it too seriously
